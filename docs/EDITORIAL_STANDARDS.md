# Editorial standards

## Product purpose

The Fact Desk is an evidence-ranked briefing desk for public-interest news
signals. It helps readers see which stories are source-backed, uncertain,
under-covered, developing, or broadly confirmed without optimizing for outrage,
virality, ideology, or clickbait.

The product promise is:

> News signals ranked by evidence, not outrage.

## What "evidence-ranked" means

Evidence-ranked does not mean "perfect truth" or automated fact-checking. It
means stories are selected and placed according to editorial judgment about:

- source spread
- primary documentation
- credibility of sourcing
- uncertainty and disputed details
- under-covered public importance
- relevance to civic, economic, health, legal, cultural, or safety decisions

## What The Fact Desk is not

The Fact Desk is not:

- a truth machine
- a partisan scoreboard
- a virality engine
- a personalized outrage feed
- a medical, legal, or financial advice product
- an automated publisher of unreviewed AI summaries

Labels are evidence posture and coverage signals, not final verdicts.

## Story lifecycle

The expected lifecycle is:

`raw feed item -> editorial inbox -> draft story -> human review -> published story -> archived/corrected story`

Raw feed items and drafts should not appear publicly. Publication requires human
review unless that policy is explicitly changed later.

## Source handling

Editors should attach sources that support the public briefing. Prefer:

- primary documents
- official statements or filings
- reputable wire or newsroom reporting
- expert analysis with transparent sourcing
- local reporting when it adds firsthand detail

Avoid presenting source quantity as proof by itself. Multiple sources can repeat
the same weak claim; one primary document may carry more evidence weight than
several derivative articles.

## Confidence levels

- **Confirmed**: core facts are well established by primary documentation or
  multiple reliable sources.
- **Developing**: credible reporting exists, but key details may change.
- **Disputed**: credible sources disagree or claims remain contested.
- **Single-source**: based on one source or one source family; public wording
  should be cautious.

## Evidence levels

- **Strong**: primary documentation, official record, or multiple independent
  reliable sources support the core facts.
- **Moderate**: credible sourcing supports the briefing, but some details remain
  developing or dependent on secondary reporting.
- **Low**: early signal, limited sourcing, single-source feed item, or material
  uncertainty remains.

Evidence level and confidence are related but not identical. Confidence describes
how settled the core facts are; evidence level describes the support behind the
briefing.

## Signal criteria

- **Top Signal**: high public relevance and sufficient evidence posture for lead
  placement.
- **Under-covered**: important public-interest story with limited mainstream
  pickup relative to impact.
- **Cross-angle**: credible sources cover the same facts with meaningfully
  different emphasis.
- **Developing**: unfolding story that should be monitored and framed with
  uncertainty.

## Under-covered signal criteria

Use "Under-covered" when a story has meaningful public relevance but limited
attention. Examples:

- local or regulatory decisions with broader consequences
- primary documents that have not been widely explained
- safety, infrastructure, health, or court signals with low pickup
- corrections or follow-ups that alter a previous public understanding

Do not use "Under-covered" merely because a story is niche; it should also have
public-interest significance.

## How uncertainty should be displayed

Every story should make uncertainty visible when material. Use the uncertainty
note to say what is not known, disputed, unverified, or dependent on future data.

Good uncertainty notes:

- "The agency has not yet published the underlying tables."
- "Only one outlet has reported the allegation."
- "Official casualty figures differ from hospital counts."
- "The rule text is proposed, not final."

Avoid vague disclaimers that do not help the reader understand the evidence
posture.

## Corrections

Corrections should be handled as editorial events, not hidden edits. When a
published story changes materially:

- preserve the corrected story record
- explain what changed
- update confidence/evidence labels if needed
- link to corrected source material when possible

The current MVP has correction status support but not a full public correction
workflow.

## Health Desk caution standard

Health Desk content covers health policy, public-health guidance, research
signals, safety flags, and source-reviewed evidence updates. It is not
personalized medical advice.

Health stories should be especially careful with:

- supplement claims
- emerging studies
- lifestyle interventions
- disease risk claims
- treatment comparisons
- causal language

Prefer primary research, guidelines, regulatory notices, and established medical
sources. Make uncertainty and limitations visible.

## AI-assistance policy for future features

AI may assist with:

- clustering related feed items
- extracting claims
- drafting neutral summaries
- identifying source spread
- suggesting confidence/evidence labels
- detecting uncertainty

AI may not:

- publish without human review
- invent facts
- fabricate sources
- replace primary source links
- present disputed information as settled
- generate sensational headlines
- obscure uncertainty

Future AI-assisted drafting must preserve source links, cite evidence posture,
and require human review before publication.

## Human review requirement

Publication should remain human-reviewed. Editors are responsible for checking:

- headline neutrality
- source support
- confidence and evidence labels
- uncertainty note
- why the story belongs on The Fact Desk
- whether the story is public-interest relevant

Automation can help prepare a briefing, but a human editor decides whether it is
ready for the public desk.
